SAMANVAY — PITCH 1 OF 3
The model, the calibration, and why the interval is the product
======================================================================


THE ONE SENTENCE
----------------------------------------------------------------------

We do not forecast electricity. We forecast the bill — and we forecast
it as a distribution, because the number that costs money is not the
average load, it is the worst likely load.


THE PROBLEM, PRECISELY
----------------------------------------------------------------------

A bulk-supply society is billed two ways. Energy, at ₹6.35 a unit,
which is the number everybody thinks about. And demand, at ₹608 per
kVA, charged on the single highest fifteen minutes of the entire
month.

One interval. Not the average, not the daily peak, not the p95 of the
month. The maximum. Every other interval in those thirty days is
billed at zero for demand.

That asymmetry is the whole business. If you flatten one fifteen
minute window you have changed the bill. If you shave the average by
ten percent and leave the peak alone, you have changed nothing.

So the unit of the product is the interval, and the day is ninety-six
of them.


WHY A POINT FORECAST CANNOT DO THIS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The naive design is: forecast the load, subtract it from the
transformer limit, allocate the difference. That controller aims at
the ceiling with a mean forecast, and a mean forecast is wrong half
the time in the upward direction. Half your errors land on top of the
transformer.

The fix is not a better mean. It is to stop using a mean.

We predict seven quantiles of society load at fifteen minute
resolution with LightGBM, put a conformal layer on top, and substitute
the ninety-fifth percentile into the capacity constraint. The
controller then plans against the worst likely load rather than the
expected one. That is a chance-constrained formulation, and it is one
line in the constraint and an entire change in behaviour.

Dispatch is deterministic and auditable. It is not a learned policy,
there is no black box between the forecast and the relay, and every
decision names the entry in the constraint registry that produced it.
That is what makes "why was I asked" answerable to a resident, and not
only to us.


THE PART MOST TEAMS SKIP: CALIBRATION
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Anyone can emit a q95. The question is whether it is a real
ninety-fifth percentile or a decoration.

Measured on 184,320 held-out intervals:

  nominal 0.05   →  empirical 0.049
  nominal 0.25   →  empirical 0.252
  nominal 0.50   →  empirical 0.508
  nominal 0.75   →  empirical 0.762
  nominal 0.95   →  empirical 0.939

  90% interval coverage:  89.0%   against a 90% target
  Acceptance test:        PASS

Those five rows are the claim. A band that says ninety-five and
delivers ninety-four is a band a controller can safely aim under. A
band that says ninety-five and delivers seventy is an overdraft the
transformer pays.

Walk-forward, never shuffled. Train 2016-01 to 2017-03, validate
2017-04 to 2017-05, test on a month the model has never seen.


THE ABLATION, WHICH IS THE ACTUAL ARGUMENT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

We built the forecaster behind an interface:

    interface Forecaster { predict(plan, sc) -> { q05, q50, q95 } }

Swap the implementation and nothing downstream moves. The optimiser,
the tariff, the physics, the simulator and the random seed are all
pinned. Only the file the quantiles come from changes. So a difference
on screen is a difference in the model, never a difference in the
drawing code.

Eight forecasters, same controller, same month:

  Ours, calibrated q95      pinball  2.397     0 breaches
  Seasonal naive            pinball  6.213     0 breaches
  Climatology               pinball  7.314     1 breach
  Static margin, no fc      pinball 20.307    11 breaches
  Persistence               pinball 27.582    87 breaches

Read the last row twice. Persistence claims the second-most headroom
in the table — it draws a narrow, confident band, so the controller
believes it and aims high. Then the evening arrives. Eighty-seven
breaches.

That is the sentence to remember: headroom without calibration is an
overdraft, not a gain.

We also report where we lose. Seasonal naive is genuinely safe on a
calm month, and it is in the table saying so. Our margin over it is
headroom and peak, not safety. A comparison table you only publish
when you win is marketing, not evidence.

Perfect foresight is in there too, as the bound — not a baseline. It
tells you how much of the achievable saving any controller could
possibly capture, so our number has a ceiling to be judged against
rather than a floor to look good over.


WHAT THE EVIDENCE IS, AND WHAT IT IS NOT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Say this before a judge asks it.

The calibration and the ablation are measured on real metered data
from a commercial office building — 13,759 sqm, 505 kVA contract
demand, all-electric cooling, chosen because it has the sharpest
demand-charge exposure on its site (p99/median = 2.9).

The residential society is a simulation. It is driven by the same
tariff, the same constraint registry and the same forecaster
interface, but it is not a claim that we have run this on sixty real
flats, because we have not.

The method is proven on real data. The deployment is not yet proven on
a real society. Those are different sentences and we keep them
different. What is next is not a bigger model — it is one society's
data-sharing agreement.


THE SEAMS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Nothing is hardcoded. Rates, limits, constraints, devices, scenarios,
the estate and the feature flags are all configuration files. The
forecaster is an interface.

A pricing change, a new tariff category, a different transformer, a
larger estate, a new stress event — each is an edit to one JSON file
and nothing else in the system moves. If answering a jury question
required editing the model, a seam was cut in the wrong place.
